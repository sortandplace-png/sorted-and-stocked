# Household Management Apps Compared

Every app in this category looks the same on a features page. They differ in exactly four ways, and only those four decide whether one survives in your house.

---

## 1. Can more than one person use it?

**This eliminates most of them.**

If your spouse, your children and whoever helps in the house can't each update it from their own phone, you haven't built a system — you've built a job, and it's yours.

Ask specifically: how many users, at what cost, and can someone update without seeing everything.

---

## 2. How long does one item take?

Watch someone add a jar of tomatoes. If it takes more than a few seconds, nobody will do it by week three.

Look for barcode scanning, photo capture, and sensible defaults. Avoid anything that requires a category, a quantity, a location and a date for every item.

---

## 3. Does it tell you anything, or just store things?

**A low-stock alert is the entire difference** between a tool and a filing cabinet.

If it can only show you what you own, you're doing data entry as a hobby.

---

## 4. Can it describe your actual house?

For a kosher kitchen this is the one that rules most of them out.

You need a meat, dairy and pareve designation on every item, visible at a glance, and locations that reflect a kitchen with two of everything. An app that treats "the kitchen" as a single place cannot describe yours, and no amount of workaround fixes that.

Multiple properties matter too if you run more than one house.

---

## How to test one properly

**Don't evaluate on the features page.** Every app looks capable there.

Take one shelf. Enter it. Add one other person. Set a low-stock level. Then use it for a fortnight and answer two questions:

**Did it stay accurate? Did anyone other than you update it?**

If the answer to the second is no, the app failed regardless of how good it looked.

---

## What to expect to give up

Free tools generally cap users, which breaks the first requirement.

Insurance-focused tools are excellent at valuables and poor at consumables. General list apps are fast and hold no structure. Purpose-built household tools do more and cost more.

**Very few handle kashrus at all**, which is why the shortlist is shorter than the category suggests.

---

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What actually differentiates household management apps?

Four things: whether more than one person can use it, how many seconds it takes to add an item, whether it tells you what's low rather than only what you own, and whether it can describe your actual house — including kashrus designations and multiple properties.

### How do I test an app before committing?

Not on the features page. Take one shelf, enter it, add one other person, set a low-stock level, and use it for a fortnight. Then answer two questions: did it stay accurate, and did anyone other than you update it. If the second is no, it failed.

### Why does multi-user access matter so much?

Because if only one person can update it, the system is that person's job forever and drifts out of date the first time someone else takes the last of something. Free tools usually cap users, which is where most of them fail.

### Do any handle a kosher kitchen properly?

Very few. You need a meat, dairy and pareve designation on every item and locations that reflect a kitchen with two of everything. An app treating 'the kitchen' as one place cannot describe yours, and no workaround fixes it.

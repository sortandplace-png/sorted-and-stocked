# How To Declutter Your Home Without Feeling Overwhelmed

You look at the closet, feel something drop in your chest, and close the door.

That's not laziness. A full closet is four hundred small decisions stacked in a row, and the deciding is what stops people — not the lifting.

So the answer is to face fewer decisions at once.

---

## Work in containers with edges

One drawer. One shelf. One box.

Not "the bedroom." Rooms have no finish line, which is why they get abandoned at sixty percent and why the abandoned version feels worse than before you started.

**Finish the small thing completely. Then stop.**

---

## Set a timer and obey it

Fifteen minutes. When it goes, stop — especially if you're not done.

Stopping with energy left is what makes tomorrow possible. Working until you're wrung out is how a project becomes a thing you avoid.

---

## Four piles, decided as you go

**Keep. Donate. Bin. Somewhere else in the house.**

That last pile is the one people forget, and it's why they wander off mid-sort and lose an hour. Put it in a box, deal with it at the end, don't leave the room.

---

## The questions that actually decide it

Run in order, stop at the first clear answer.

**Is it broken?** Then it goes. No further thought.

**Have I used it in a year?** If no, strong candidate.

**Would I buy this again today, at full price?** This is the one that does the work — it moves you from *what did I pay* to *what is this worth to me now*, and only the second one affects anything going forward.

**Do I have a better one?** Keep the better one.

---

## Leave the hard things for last

Photographs. Anything inherited. Gifts from people who'll notice.

Deciding genuinely gets faster with practice. Twenty minutes of matching lids to containers makes you better at the box of your grandmother's things — not because you care less, but because you've stopped agonising over every call.

**And if something is too hard right now, box it, date it, revisit in a year.** That's a decision too, and it beats forcing one you'll regret.

---

## Get it out the same day

A donation bag left in the hallway gets unpacked. Into the car, or gone.

Decide the destination before you start, or the piles just relocate.

---

## Then stop it coming back

**One in, one out** on the categories that creep — clothes, toys, books, kitchen things.

**Every item that stayed needs a home.** Things without one become the next pile within weeks.

**Fifteen minutes in the evening.** Anything that migrated goes back. That's what stops the whole house ever needing rescuing.

---

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why does decluttering feel so overwhelming?

Because a full closet is hundreds of small decisions stacked together, and the deciding is what exhausts people rather than the physical work. The fix is facing fewer decisions at once — one drawer or one shelf rather than a whole room.

### How long should I declutter for at a time?

Fifteen minutes, and stop when the timer goes even if you're not finished. Stopping with energy left is what makes it possible to come back tomorrow; working until you're wrung out turns the project into something you avoid.

### What questions help me decide whether to keep something?

In order: is it broken, have I used it in a year, would I buy it again today at full price, and do I have a better one. The third does most of the work — it moves you from what you paid to what it's worth now, which is the only part that affects anything going forward.

### What should I do with sentimental items?

Leave them until last. Deciding gets measurably faster with practice, so twenty minutes on easy categories makes the hard ones easier. If something is genuinely too hard, box it, date it and revisit in a year — that's a decision too.

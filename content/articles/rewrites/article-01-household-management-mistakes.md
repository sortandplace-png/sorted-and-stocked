# 10 Common Household Management Mistakes (and How to Fix Them)

A large home does not become difficult to run because anyone is careless. It becomes difficult because the information required to run it is scattered across a dozen places, and none of those places can be checked by anyone except the person who put it there.

The mistakes below are not dramatic. Each one is small, repeated, and quietly expensive. What follows is what each one actually costs, and what to do instead.

## The System Itself

### 1. Running the house on memory and text threads

Verbal instructions and scattered messages disappear the moment the conversation moves on. Every question has to be answered again the next time it comes up, usually by the one person who cannot delegate it.

- **The cost:** Every question routes to one person. That person becomes the bottleneck, and the household stalls whenever they are travelling, unwell, or simply in a meeting.
- **The fix:** One searchable source of truth that anyone with the right access can check. The goal is that a staff member consults a system, not a person.

### 2. Waiting to be organized before starting

There is a persistent instinct to get the house in order first, then document it. It reverses the actual sequence.

- **The cost:** The documentation never begins, because the house is never finished.
- **The fix:** Start from wherever you are. A half-complete record that exists beats a perfect one that does not.

### 3. Cataloguing the entire property on day one

The impulse is to log everything at once, across every room, in a single weekend.

- **The cost:** The effort collapses around day three, and the abandoned attempt makes the next one harder to start.
- **The fix:** Begin where friction is highest. The pantry, the fridge, the freezer, the main supply closets. Momentum matters more than completeness in the first month.

> Most households we work with have tried at least one of these on their own. The system is rarely the hard part. Sustaining it is. That is generally where we come in.

## Information and Standards

### 4. No consistent name for anything

The same item is recorded three ways by three people, so nothing can be searched and nothing can be counted.

- **The cost:** Duplicate purchases, phantom shortages, and a list nobody trusts.
- **The fix:** One agreed name per item, written down once, used everywhere.

### 5. Instructions that say what, but never how

A task list tells staff which jobs exist. It rarely tells them what finished looks like.

- **The cost:** Work is completed to five different standards, and the correction conversation repeats monthly.
- **The fix:** A written procedure per recurring task, with a clear definition of done. Photographs where words are ambiguous.

### 6. Documentation in a language the staff does not read

This is the most common oversight in homes with household staff, and the most consequential.

- **The cost:** Instructions exist and are not used. The written standard becomes decorative, and the household falls back on demonstration and correction.
- **The fix:** Every procedure available in the languages actually spoken in the house. Written at the point of creation, not translated later as a project that never quite happens.

### 7. Warranties and manuals in a drawer

Appliance paperwork is filed physically, in the one location nobody thinks to check under pressure.

- **The cost:** Covered repairs paid for out of pocket. Replacements bought while the original was still under warranty.
- **The fix:** Model number, purchase date, and coverage window recorded against each appliance, searchable in seconds.

> Vendors, warranties, and staff procedures are the three areas where the return appears fastest. They are also the three that tend to be built once and then left to age.

## People and Continuity

### 8. Maintenance that lives in someone's head

Filters, gutters, seasonal changeovers, and service intervals are remembered rather than scheduled.

- **The cost:** The task is noticed by its consequence rather than by the calendar, and the consequence is always more expensive.
- **The fix:** Recurring tasks with real intervals, visible to more than one person.

### 9. One person holding every vendor relationship

The plumber, the electrician, and the preferred grocery contact all exist in a single phone.

- **The cost:** An urgent problem waits for that person to be reachable.
- **The fix:** A vendor directory with names, numbers, and a note on who is approved to enter the house.

### 10. No plan for the week you are unreachable

Every household eventually tests this, and rarely on a convenient schedule.

- **The cost:** The system was never a system. It was one person, and the house discovers this at the worst possible moment.
- **The fix:** Ask directly whether the household could run for a week without you. The honest answer identifies exactly what to document first.

## Where to Begin

If several of these are familiar, that is the ordinary condition of a busy home rather than a failure of management. The households that run well are not the ones with the most help. They are the ones where the information is written down, current, and reachable by the people who need it.

Pick the one that would cost you most this month. Fix that. The rest gets easier once something is written down and someone other than you has checked it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the most common household management mistake?

Running the house on memory and text threads. Every question routes back to one person, and that person becomes the bottleneck for every decision, vendor call, and new hire's first three months.

### Where should I start if several of these apply?

Start with vendor contacts and emergency shutoffs. Both take an afternoon, neither requires a system, and together they remove the sharpest risk of a household that depends on one person being reachable.

### Why do written staff instructions so often go unused?

Usually because they are written in a language the staff does not read, or because they say what to do without saying what finished looks like. Both send the household back to demonstration and correction, which is the cost the instructions were meant to remove.

### How do I know if my household system is actually working?

Ask whether the house could run for a week without you. The answer identifies exactly what to document first, and it is a more useful test than how organized anything looks.

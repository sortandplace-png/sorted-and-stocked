# How To Run Your Home Like A CEO

The phrase usually means colour-coded calendars and a laminated morning routine. That isn't what a CEO does.

A CEO doesn't do the work. They decide what work happens, who owns it, what it costs, and whether it's worth continuing.

Running your home that way isn't about doing housework better. It's about being the person who decides how the house runs.

---

## The test

If you were unreachable for two weeks, would the house drift or just be inconvenienced?

Drift means you're operating it. Inconvenience means you own it.

---

## Decide what you stop doing

Every household accumulates obligations nobody ever chose. Ironed sheets. Homemade everything. A spotless house before guests.

Go through them and ask: **if this weren't already happening, would I start it today?**

Some are worth every hour. Some are being kept because stopping feels like losing.

**You're allowed to lower a standard on purpose.** That's a decision, not a failure, and it's the highest-return one available.

---

## Build, buy, or skip

For every recurring job there are three options and most households only consider the first.

**Build** — you or your family do it. **Buy** — someone else does. **Skip** — it stops.

The calculation isn't only money. Grocery delivery costs a fee and removes both the trip and the weekly logistics of fitting it in. The second part is usually worth more.

Worth running the numbers on: groceries, cleaning, laundry in heavy seasons, lawn and snow, meal components, errands, and anything you dread.

**And the reverse.** Some things you currently pay for would be better skipped entirely.

---

## The honest org chart

Write down every recurring category of household work. Next to each, the name of the person who **owns** it.

Not who helps. Who owns — noticing it needs doing, deciding when, doing it or arranging it, knowing when supplies run low.

**The noticing is the heavy part**, and it's what stays with you when you hand over a task instead of a category.

Most people doing this find their own name beside nearly everything, including things they'd have sworn were shared.

**With a spouse:** name specific complete categories. *"I want to hand off these three entirely, including the remembering. Which do you take?"* That's answerable in a way "I need more help" isn't.

**With children:** one complete task beats five they assist with.

**Then accept their standard.** Eighty-five percent permanently beats a hundred percent done by you resentfully.

---

## Know what the house costs

Most households know the mortgage and roughly the groceries, and after that it's fog.

Write down the recurring categories — food, supplies, services, maintenance with a real reserve, children, staff, seasonal.

Two things fall out immediately.

**The invisible spend.** Duplicate purchases because you couldn't see what you had. Food thrown out. Rush buying at full price. Subscriptions nobody uses. None dramatic; together, substantial.

**Seasonal reality.** Yom Tov and simcha seasons carry costs that annual averages hide completely. A household running on monthly averages gets surprised every year by something entirely predictable. **Budget the seasons as seasons.**

---

## Hire into a defined role

The common mistake isn't hiring the wrong person. It's hiring anyone without documentation, which converts household labour into management labour and leaves you just as busy, now supervising.

Before anyone starts: written scope, written standards, written procedures for anything with a right and wrong way, and a defined way to flag problems and low supplies.

**Bilingually where the household needs it.** English-only documentation handed to someone who reads Spanish more comfortably guarantees errors that get blamed on the person rather than the page.

**The measure of good help isn't how well they follow instructions. It's how few they need** — and that's documentation, not the person.

---

## Review on a schedule

**Weekly, twenty minutes.** Calendar, meals, list, anything unusual.

**Monthly, thirty.** What's being ignored. Anything ignored a full month needs cutting down or deleting.

**Quarterly, ninety.** Does the org chart still hold or has it drifted back? What did we spend? What would we stop if starting fresh? What's coming that needs deciding now?

**The cadence is the whole discipline.** Without it you're reacting, which is the operator's job.

---

## What this isn't

**Not treating your family like employees.** The org chart describes work, not relationships.

**Not optimizing everything.** Some things at home are meant to be inefficient — the long Friday afternoon, the meal that takes longer than it needs to. Protect those deliberately.

**Not more systems.** Half of this is doing less.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What does it mean to run your home like a CEO?

Deciding how the house runs rather than performing the work better — what the household stops doing, what gets bought rather than absorbed, who owns each category including the noticing, what it costs, and reviewing all of that on a schedule instead of reacting daily.

### How do I hand off household work so it doesn't come back?

Hand over complete categories rather than tasks, and say explicitly that ownership includes noticing it needs doing and knowing when supplies run low. "Help me with the laundry" leaves the tracking with you, and the tracking is the heavier half. Then accept the other person's standard.

### Should I budget by household category?

Yes, and separate the seasons from the monthly average. Most households can't say what it costs to run the house, which hides both the invisible spend from duplicate buying and waste, and the fact that Yom Tov and simcha seasons carry predictable costs that averaging conceals.

### How often should I review how the household runs?

Weekly for twenty minutes, monthly for thirty to catch what's being ignored, and quarterly for ninety to revisit ownership, spend and standards. The cadence is the actual discipline — without it everything reverts to reacting.

### Isn't running a home like a business cold?

It would be if it meant treating people like employees, which it doesn't — the org chart describes work, not relationships. And much of it is doing less rather than more: cutting obligations, lowering standards that return nothing, and deliberately protecting the parts of home life meant to be inefficient.

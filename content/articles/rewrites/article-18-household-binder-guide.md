# The Ultimate Household Binder Guide

Somebody rings the doorbell and it's the plumber you forgot you booked. Or the boiler dies on a Friday afternoon. Or your husband needs the insurance policy number and you're not home.

A household binder is where the answer to all of those lives, so it isn't only in your head.

---

## What actually goes in it

Not everything. The binder gets abandoned when it tries to hold the whole house.

**Emergency information.** Who to call and in what order. Doctor, pediatrician, poison control, the neighbour with a spare key. Where the water main, gas shut-off and electrical panel are — with photographs, because "in the basement" is useless to a plumber on the phone.

**The house itself.** Every major appliance: brand, model number, serial number, rough age. HVAC filter size. Fridge water filter model. **Photograph the model plate on each one** — it's always on a sticker behind something, and every repair call starts with that number.

**Vendors.** Plumber, electrician, HVAC, handyman, appliance repair, lawn, snow. Names and numbers, gathered before you need them. Finding a plumber at 11pm with water coming through a ceiling is not the moment to be reading reviews.

**Documents you'd need in a hurry.** Insurance policy numbers, not the policies. Account references. Where the passports and birth certificates actually are.

**How things get done here.** The Shabbos timer settings. What "the kitchen is clean" means. Which cabinet the good dishes go in. The things you'd otherwise explain out loud for the fortieth time.

**Written in Spanish too if that's what your staff reads.** A procedure nobody can read isn't a procedure.

---

## What to leave out

Anything that changes weekly. Meal plans, shopping lists, the school calendar — those live where they're used, not in a binder.

Anything you'd never look up. If you can't picture the moment you'd open to that page, it doesn't earn the space.

**A binder full of things nobody consults is worse than no binder**, because the useful pages get lost among them.

---

## Paper or digital

Paper wins for emergencies — it works when the power's out and anyone can pick it up.

Digital wins for updating and for having a copy that survives the house.

**Most households want both:** a slim physical binder in a known place with the emergency and shut-off pages, and everything else digital. Keep a copy of the digital version somewhere other than the house.

---

## Build it in an hour

Don't set out to make a complete one. Make a useful one.

**Emergency page first.** Numbers, shut-off locations, photographs. That's twenty minutes and it's the page that justifies the whole thing.

**Then appliances.** Walk around, photograph every model plate. Twenty minutes.

**Then vendors.** Whoever you've used, whoever your neighbours recommend. Twenty minutes.

Stop there. You now have a binder that would genuinely help on a bad day. Add procedures over the following weeks, one at a time, as you notice yourself explaining something for the second time.

---

## Keeping it true

A binder that's out of date is worse than none, because someone will act on it.

**Review it twice a year.** Ten minutes. New appliances added, dead numbers removed, anything that changed.

**Update it at the moment things change**, not later — new dishwasher means the model number goes in that day, while the paperwork's still on the counter.

**Tell everyone it exists and where.** A binder only you know about is a binder that doesn't exist.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What should go in a household binder?

Emergency contacts, photographed locations of the water main, gas shut-off and electrical panel, appliance model and serial numbers, a vendor list, references for key documents, and the procedures that have a right and wrong way in your house. Not meal plans or anything that changes weekly.

### Should a household binder be paper or digital?

Both, usually. Paper works when the power is out and anyone can pick it up, which is what you need in an emergency. Digital is easier to keep current and survives the house itself. A slim physical binder for the emergency pages, everything else digital with a copy stored elsewhere.

### How do I make one without it taking all weekend?

Build the emergency page, then photograph every appliance model plate, then list your vendors. Twenty minutes each. Stop there — you'll have something that genuinely helps on a bad day. Add procedures later, one at a time, whenever you catch yourself explaining something twice.

### How often should I update it?

Review twice a year for ten minutes, and update at the moment something changes rather than afterwards. An out-of-date binder is worse than none, because somebody will act on it.

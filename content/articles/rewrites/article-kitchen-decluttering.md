# Kitchen Decluttering: How To Let Go Of What You Dont Use

You know the pasta machine isn't earning its shelf. You've known for four years.

It's not that you can't see it. It's that getting rid of it means admitting the version of you who was going to make fresh pappardelle didn't turn up.

Kitchen decluttering is almost never a knowledge problem. It's that these particular objects carry more than function.

---

## Why kitchen things are harder

**The money is visible.** You remember what the mixer cost.

The reframe: **the money left when you bought it.** Keeping the item doesn't recover a penny — it just adds storage cost on top. The only live question is whether it earns its space now.

**It represents a version of you.** The bread machine, the juicer, the tagine. Letting go can feel like conceding the life. It isn't — you can decide to bake bread next year and buy a machine then. **The equipment doesn't hold the intention. You do.**

**Someone gave it to you.** The gift completed its purpose when it was given. Storing something unused doesn't honour anyone; it stores obligation.

**It's food-adjacent.** There's a particular resistance to anything connected with feeding people. Worth naming so you can set it aside — a cracked container is not food.

---

## Work in this order

Easiest to hardest, deliberately. You'll make better decisions on the wedding china after twenty minutes of practice on plastic lids.

**Containers first.** Match every lid to a base; anything unmatched goes, both directions. Then keep one system — four brands that don't stack is why the cupboard is chaos.

**Duplicate utensils.** Two of anything you use daily, one of everything else.

**Chipped and mismatched.** Would you serve a guest on it? If not, it isn't really in your kitchen — it's occupying space in it.

**Single-purpose gadgets.** Used in the last year? If not, a knife does the job. Exception: if it makes something you cook *often* meaningfully easier, it's earned its place.

**Appliances you don't use weekly.** If it's genuinely seasonal but real — keep it and move it out of the kitchen. A labelled bin in a hall closet costs nothing and frees prime space. People forget that's an option.

**Wedding gifts and inherited pieces last.** Keep one piece rather than a set. Photograph it and release it. Pass it to someone who'll use it. Or use it — good china used twice a year is doing its job; good china in a box for twelve years isn't.

**And if something is genuinely too hard right now, box it, date it, revisit in a year.** That's a decision too.

---

## For a kosher kitchen

Work one designation at a time — fleishig fully, then milchig, then pareve — rather than moving between them. Cleaner decisions, and no risk of mixing during the sort.

Duplicates within a designation are still duplicates. Yom Tov and Pesach sets are legitimately seasonal: contained and stored outside the kitchen, not discarded.

Any question about the status of an item, or whether something can be given away, goes to your Rav.

---

## Get it out the same day

Decide the destination before you start or the piles just relocate. Into the car, or gone.

Then **one in, one out** on cookware, gadgets and containers, and a five-minute weekly pass so it doesn't need doing again.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Where should I start decluttering my kitchen?

Food storage containers. Match every lid to a base and discard both orphans and anything warped or stained. It's the easiest category, carries no emotional weight, frees the most volume, and gives you twenty minutes of decision practice before anything harder.

### How do I let go of expensive equipment I don't use?

The money left when you bought it — keeping the item recovers nothing and adds a storage cost on top. The only live question is whether it earns its space now. Ask whether you'd buy it again today at full price.

### What about wedding gifts I've never used?

The gift completed its purpose when it was given. Storing something unused stores obligation, not gratitude. Keep one piece rather than a set, pass it to a family member who'll use it, or photograph it and let it go.

### How do I declutter a kosher kitchen?

One designation at a time — fleishig fully, then milchig, then pareve — rather than moving between them, labelling as you sort. Duplicates within a designation are still duplicates. Yom Tov and Pesach sets should be contained and stored outside the kitchen rather than discarded, and questions of status go to your Rav.

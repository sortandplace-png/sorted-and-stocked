# How To Run Your Home Like A CEO

The phrase gets used badly. It usually means colour-coded calendars and a laminated morning routine — a productivity aesthetic bolted onto ordinary domestic work.

That's not what a CEO does.

A CEO doesn't do the work. They decide what work happens, who does it, what it costs, and whether it's worth continuing. They own outcomes, not tasks. And critically, **they're evaluated on whether the organization functions when they're not in the room.**

To **manage your home like a CEO** is to change your relationship to the work rather than to optimize how you perform it. It means deciding what your household does and doesn't do, what you buy out rather than absorb, who owns what completely, and what the household costs to run — then reviewing all of that on a schedule instead of reacting to it daily.

This guide covers the role, not the toolkit: the decisions a household executive actually makes, the honest org chart, the budget nobody writes down, hiring and managing help, and the quarterly review that turns a household from something you cope with into something you direct.

## The Shift: Operator to Owner

Most household managers are operating. Doing the tasks, holding the information, being the point of escalation for everything.

An owner does something different. Four things, specifically:

1. **Decides what the household does at all** — and what it stops doing
2. **Decides what gets bought out** rather than absorbed
3. **Assigns ownership** of complete categories, including the noticing
4. **Reviews on a cadence** rather than reacting continuously

None of that is about doing housework better. It's about being the person who decides how the house runs, which is a different job — and it's the one that produces a household capable of functioning without you.

**The test:** if you were unreachable for two weeks, what would happen? An operator's absence causes drift. An owner's absence is inconvenient.

## Decision One: What Does This Household Not Do?

Every organization has things it has stopped doing. Most households never make that decision explicitly, so they accumulate obligations by default and then wonder why the week is full.

**Run the audit.** List everything your household currently does that takes recurring time — activities, commitments, standards, traditions, obligations. Then, for each:

**If this weren't already happening, would I start it today?**

Anything that gets a clear no is a candidate to stop. Not everything with a no should go — some obligations are genuine — but the exercise separates *chosen* from *inherited*.

**Standards count too, and this is the harder half.** Ironed sheets. Homemade everything. A spotless house before guests. Handmade costumes. Each is a standard someone chose, often years ago, that now runs automatically.

An executive asks: **what does this standard cost, and what does it return?** Some are worth every hour. Some are being maintained purely because stopping feels like a defeat.

**You are allowed to lower a standard on purpose.** That's a decision, not a failure — and it's one of the highest-return decisions available.

## Decision Two: Build, Buy, or Skip

For every recurring piece of household work, there are three options. Most households only consider the first.

**Build** — you or your family do it.
**Buy** — you pay someone or something else to.
**Skip** — it stops happening.

**The calculation isn't only money.** It's money against time, and against the decision load. Grocery delivery costs a fee and removes both the trip *and* the weekly logistics of fitting it in. The second part is frequently worth more than the first.

Categories worth running the calculation on:

- Grocery shopping and delivery
- Cleaning
- Laundry, at least in heavy seasons
- Lawn, snow, and exterior maintenance
- Meal components, or full meals during hard stretches
- Errands and returns
- Anything you dread

**The honest version of the question:** if this hour cost me what my time is actually worth, would I still spend it here?

**And the reverse also applies.** Some things you currently buy would be better skipped entirely — a subscription nobody uses, a service filling a need that no longer exists. Executives cut spend as readily as they add it.

## Decision Three: The Honest Org Chart

Write down every recurring category of household work. Then, beside each, the name of the person who **owns** it.

Not who helps. Who owns.

Ownership means: noticing it needs doing, deciding when, doing it or arranging it, and knowing when supplies run low. **The noticing is the heavy part** — and the part that stays with you when you delegate a task rather than a category.

Most people doing this exercise discover their name beside nearly everything, including categories they'd have sworn were shared. That's the finding. It's not an accusation of anyone; it's a description of how households drift.

### Reassigning

**With a spouse:** name specific complete categories and ask which they want. *"I want to hand off these three entirely, including the remembering. Which do you take?"* That's answerable in a way "I need more help" isn't.

**With children:** one complete age-appropriate task beats five they assist with. A ten-year-old who owns the recycling — noticing, sorting, taking it out, telling you when bags run low — has learned something a chore chart doesn't teach.

**Then accept their standard.** Delegated work at 85%, permanently, beats work at 100% done by you resentfully. If a category genuinely requires 100%, keep it — but be honest about how few actually do.

## Decision Four: The Household Budget Nobody Writes

Businesses know their operating costs. Most households know their mortgage and roughly what they spend on groceries, and after that it's a fog.

An executive can answer: **what does it cost to run this house per month, and where is that money going?**

Write down the recurring categories:

- Food — groceries, delivery, eating out, separated
- Household supplies and consumables
- Services — cleaning, lawn, laundry, subscriptions
- Maintenance — the recurring, plus a genuine reserve for the unplanned
- Children — activities, school, clothing, equipment
- Staff, if you employ any
- Seasonal — Yom Tov, simchas, holidays, gifts

**Two things fall out of this immediately.**

**First, the invisible spend.** Duplicate purchases because you couldn't see what you had, food thrown away because it wasn't visible, rush buying at full price, subscriptions nobody uses. None of it is dramatic. Together, across a year, it's substantial — and it's the single strongest financial argument for a maintained inventory.

**Second, seasonal reality.** Yom Tov and simcha seasons carry costs that annual averages hide completely. A household running on monthly averages is surprised every year by something entirely predictable. **Budget the seasons as seasons.**

## Decision Five: Hiring and Managing Help

The most common mistake households make with help isn't hiring the wrong person. It's hiring anyone at all without documentation — which converts household labour into management labour and leaves the owner just as busy, now supervising.

An executive hires into a defined role.

**Before anyone starts:**
- Written scope — what this role covers, and what it doesn't
- Written standards — what "done" looks like, specifically
- Written procedures for anything with a right way and a wrong way
- A defined way to report problems and flag low supplies
- Handover notes between shifts, so information survives staff turnover

**Written bilingually where the household needs it.** If the people running your home read Spanish more comfortably than English, English-only documentation guarantees errors that get attributed to the person rather than to the document. That's both unfair and unfixable.

**Then manage like a manager, not a supervisor.** Clear expectations, a regular check-in, feedback that's specific and timely, and genuine authority within the defined scope. Someone who has to ask you before every decision isn't reducing your load.

**The measure of good household help isn't how well they follow instructions. It's how few instructions they need** — and that's a function of documentation, not of the person.

## The Cadence: Reviewing Instead of Reacting

Executives don't manage continuously. They manage on a schedule, and the schedule is what stops everything becoming urgent.

**Weekly — 20 minutes.** The operating review. Calendar for the week, meals planned, list built, anything unusual flagged. This is the single non-negotiable.

**Monthly — 30 minutes.** What's working, what's being ignored, what needs simplifying. Anything ignored for a full month either needs cutting down or deleting. Skim the spend.

**Quarterly — 90 minutes.** The real one:

- Does the org chart still hold, or has everything drifted back?
- What did we spend, and on what?
- What are we doing that we'd stop if we were starting fresh?
- What should we now buy out that we're still absorbing?
- What's coming next quarter that needs deciding now — a season, a simcha, a school change?
- What broke repeatedly, and what would fix it permanently?

**Annually.** The bigger reset. Standards, obligations, services, insurance, systems, and whether the household's shape still matches the family's actual life.

**The cadence is the whole discipline.** Without it you're back to reacting, which is the operator's job — and the reason the week always feels full.

## What This Isn't

Worth saying plainly, because the CEO framing gets used badly.

**It isn't treating your family like employees.** The org chart is about work, not relationships. Your children owning tasks is education; your spouse owning categories is partnership. Neither is management.

**It isn't optimizing everything.** Some things in a home are meant to be inefficient — the long Friday afternoon, the meal that takes longer than it needs to, the mess a project makes. An executive protects those deliberately rather than letting them get squeezed by work that could have been delegated.

**It isn't more systems.** Half of this is about doing *less*: fewer obligations, lower standards where they don't return anything, work bought out or stopped.

**And it isn't a personality.** You don't have to become a different kind of person. You have to make about six decisions and then review them four times a year.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What does it mean to manage your home like a CEO?

It means changing your relationship to the work rather than optimizing how you perform it: deciding what your household does and doesn't do, what gets bought out rather than absorbed, who owns each category completely including the noticing, and what the house costs to run — then reviewing those decisions on a schedule instead of reacting daily.

### How is this different from a household management system?

A household management system is the structure — schedules, inventories, routines, documented procedures. The CEO framing is about the role: the decisions you make about what the household does at all, who owns what, what you spend, and what you stop. The system is the machinery; this is the direction.

### What decisions should a household executive actually make?

Five: what the household stops doing, what gets built versus bought versus skipped, who owns each category of work, what the household costs to run by category, and how help is hired and managed. Then a weekly, monthly, quarterly, and annual review cadence to keep those decisions current.

### How do I hand off household work without it coming back to me?

Hand off complete categories rather than tasks, and make explicit that ownership includes noticing it needs doing and knowing when supplies run low. "Help me with the laundry" leaves the tracking with you, and the tracking is the heavier half. Then accept the other person's standard rather than correcting to yours.

### Should I write a household budget by category?

Yes, and separate seasonal costs from monthly averages. Most households can't answer what it costs to run the house per month, which hides two things: the invisible spend from duplicate purchases and waste, and the fact that Yom Tov and simcha seasons carry predictable costs that annual averaging conceals.

### How often should I review how my household runs?

Weekly for twenty minutes as an operating review, monthly for thirty to check what's being ignored, quarterly for ninety minutes to revisit ownership, spend, and standards, and annually for the bigger reset. The cadence is the actual discipline — without it, everything reverts to reacting.

### Isn't running your home like a business cold?

It would be if it meant treating people like employees, which it doesn't. The org chart describes work, not relationships. And a good deal of the approach is about doing less rather than more — cutting obligations, lowering standards that return nothing, and deliberately protecting the parts of home life that are meant to be inefficient.
